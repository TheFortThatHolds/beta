## Chapter One — The Lamp

The lamp had gone out at some point in the night, and Ida had not noticed.

She knew this the way you know a thing you have been refusing to know: all at once, and with
the sense that some quieter part of you had been holding the news for hours, waiting for a
polite moment. The room was the wrong colour. The window over the sink, which at four in the
morning should have been throwing a slow yellow sweep across the far wall — one, two, three,
dark, and again — was throwing nothing at all.

Ida put down her cup. It made a sound against the saucer that seemed enormous.

Forty-one years her father had kept the light, and in forty-one years it had failed twice, and
both times he had been standing under it when it happened. He used to say that was the whole
job. Not the polishing, not the log, not the endless brass. Standing under it. Being the person
who noticed.

She counted the stairs on the way up, the way she had as a girl. Ninety-six. She had counted
them the summer she was eleven and had never once in the decades since gone up without the
number arriving unbidden at the top, like a receipt.

## Chapter Two — What the Sea Keeps

The lens room was cold in a way the rest of the house never was.

It was the glass, her father said. All that glass, and the sea on three sides of it, and no
amount of stove could argue with that arrangement. He had liked the cold. He had liked most of
the things about the tower that other people found unbearable, which was, Ida thought, the
single fact that explained him best.

She found the wick collapsed into itself, a small black ruin, and the reservoir dry.

Dry.

She stood with her hand flat on the frame and worked out what that meant. It meant she had not
filled it. It meant that yesterday, at some point between the post and the weather log and the
long grey middle of the afternoon, she had walked past the one task the entire building existed
for, and had not seen it, and had gone to bed.

Down at Kestrel Point the water does a particular thing on an ebb tide. It goes quiet and it
goes flat and it looks, from a boat, exactly like the deep channel, and it is not the deep
channel. It is eleven feet of shelving rock with a temper.

There had been no boats. She checked the log twice and then went out onto the gallery in her
nightdress and checked the whole black rim of the world with her father's glasses, and there
had been no boats.

That was luck. She made herself say the word out loud, into the wind, because she knew what
she would do with it otherwise — she would file it under *fine*, and *fine* was how a person
ended up doing this again in November.

## Chapter Three — The Boat Shed

Her sister arrived on the Thursday, which was three days early and entirely typical.

"You didn't call," Ruth said, from the doorway, with her bag still on her shoulder.

"There wasn't anything to call about."

"Mrs. Enfield called."

Ida turned back to the stove. "Mrs. Enfield has a telescope and a grievance, and she has never
in her life been able to tell the difference between them."

Ruth put the bag down. She did it slowly, the way she did everything when she had decided to be
patient at somebody, and Ida felt the old familiar urge to say something unforgivable just to
get the rest of the conversation over with faster.

"The light was out," Ruth said. "On Monday. For most of a night."

"Yes."

"Ida."

"I said yes." She set the pan down harder than she meant to. "I'm not going to stand here and
be told a thing I already know by somebody who found it out from a woman with a telescope."

The kettle started up, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the kettle did the
talking, and out past the window the water at the Point lay flat and quiet and lying about
its depth, the way it always had.
